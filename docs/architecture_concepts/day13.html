Monolith vs Microservices

The missing third option: a well-structured monolith beats a poorly-structured microservices architecture every single time.

1
The Proof is in Production
Shopify

Processes Black Friday on a Rails monolith. $11.5 billion in sales over one weekend. They call it the "majestic monolith." Still one of the most scalable ecommerce platforms on earth.

Amazon Prime Video

Moved video quality monitoring from microservices to monolith. 90% cost reduction. Distributed system overhead was greater than the coordination overhead it was supposed to solve.

Segment

Rebuilt their monolith after 140+ microservices. Drew wrong boundaries in year 1. Refactoring 140 services was so painful they started over. Boundaries must be earned, not invented.

Stack Overflow

1.3 billion pageviews/month from just 9 web servers, running a single .NET monolith with modular structure. More traffic than most microservices shops ever reach.

Also: GitHub, Basecamp — all monoliths at massive scale. The narrative that microservices = scale is simply wrong.

2
3 Reasons to Start with a Monolith

① You Don't Know Your Boundaries

Moving a boundary in a monolith = refactor (days). Moving across services = re-splitting DBs, redoing APIs, multi-quarter migration.

Segment's lesson: They drew wrong boundaries in year 1. Refactoring 140 services was so painful they rebuilt the monolith from scratch.

② Bottleneck is Shipping Speed

At 10k DAU, Postgres + single app server handles the load comfortably. Microservices slow down shipping 2–3x while you build deploy pipelines, debug service mesh issues, and pay the operational tax.

③ Operational Tax Compounds

5 engineers maintaining 20 services = 4 services per engineer. Each engineer becomes a part-time platform team. Feature velocity craters. The math doesn't work below 50–80 engineers.

3
The Missing Third Option: Modular Monolith
The Majestic Monolith

One deployable unit, one repo, one CI/CD, one DB server — but with strong internal module boundaries, clear team ownership per module, and independent testability per module. Gets you 80% of microservices benefits without the painful 20%.

What You Get (80%)

  • Team autonomy per module
  • Clean boundaries and ownership
  • Independent testability
  • Can refactor without network boundaries

What You Avoid (the painful 20%)

  • Network failures between services
  • Distributed transactions / saga patterns
  • Debugging across 12 service boundaries
  • 15 deploy pipelines, 15 dashboards
4
When to Extract a Service (Real Pain)

Wait until you feel the real pain. Extracting prematurely is the most common and costly mistake.

📈

① Independent Scaling Needed

Search needs 80 instances, admin needs 2. If infrastructure savings are 30%+ = worth the operational cost. Not just "search is slower" — meaningful infrastructure cost difference.

🛡

② Different Reliability Requirements

Payments needs 99.99% uptime. Reporting can be down 4 hours. Coupling them means a reporting bug takes down payments. Separation is about blast radius, not convenience.

🚀

③ Deploy Cadence Killing Velocity

"Can we deploy today?" Slack thread with 15 people, 3 teams, coordination calls every Tuesday. When deploy coordination becomes a recurring meeting, it's time to extract.

🔧

④ Genuinely Different Tech Stack

ML inference requires Python + GPUs. Your main app is Rails. This is a real technical constraint — the language boundary forces a service boundary. Extract only when the stack truly can't co-exist.

5
Bad Reasons to Microservices
❌ NOT a Reason to Extract
  • "We have 25 engineers" — headcount alone is not a signal
  • "It looks more serious / professional" — architectural LARP
  • "The CTO read a microservices book" — architecture by resume
  • "Stripe does it / Netflix does it" — they have 10,000 engineers and 20 years of domain knowledge

Extract ONE service, live with it for 3–6 months, pay the operational tax, learn what production microservices feel like. Then decide. Most teams stop at 3–5 services.

6
Honest Decision Framework
SituationRecommendationWhy
Early stage / 1 team Monolith Boundaries unknown, shipping speed matters most
Growing, 2-4 teams, deployment conflicts Modular Monolith Get 80% benefits without distributed systems pain
Specific component needs different scale/reliability/stack Extract 1 Service Feel the tax, validate the tradeoff before more extractions
Multiple teams, deploy coordination hell, proven boundaries Microservices Real organizational pain with known, stable domain boundaries
Microservices with wrong boundaries / too many services Merge Back Segment did it. Amazon did it. It's okay to learn and reverse.
The Framework

Default to modular monolith → Extract when you feel real pain → Build prerequisites first → Accept you might be wrong and need to merge back
